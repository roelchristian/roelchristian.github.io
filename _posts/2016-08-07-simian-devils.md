title: "Simian devils"
---

The Monkey King is the ageless Sun Wukong. Unlike the West, which saw the
personification (_viz._ concretisation, de-abstraction, or whatever) of evil in
serpents, dragons, or some other fierce but ultimately stupid beast, the Chinese
saw it in monkeys. They are perverted images of ourselves. I can hear K'ung
saying: ‘This is what happens when you succumb to evil; this is what happens
when you nourish evil in your hearts.’[^conf]

But this assessment is not entirely true. Sun Wukong is not the villain (he is,
in a recent Cantonese flick, but only until he begins his journey west).
Schumacher summarises the role of monkeys in Eastern myth: the monkey begins
vile and vain, but ends by discovering wisdom. The discovery often involves the
Buddha and, perversely, it does not involve shedding the incomplete human form
for one fuller and more complete. Sun Wukong remains a monkey to the end. Borges
writes of another Chinese monkey, mischievous but perhaps also adorable, whose
only pleasure was to drink the ink from a scholar’s inkpot.

But there is another monkey, undocumented except in the tortured dreams of
Nietzsche and his disciples, which is not so much a monkey as a gargoyle we
unconsciously create from our deepest fears: the monkey we alone can see, and
which we see only every morning, before we open our eyes, in front of the
mirror; the monkey which tells us, _monstrum in fronte, monstrum in animo_.

[^conf]: Here a common impulse of that time: Confucius, pressed into service as
    a ventriloquist’s dummy for a sermon that is not quite his. (Feb. 2026)
